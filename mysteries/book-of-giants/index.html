The Dead Sea Scrolls · Qumran

The Book of Giants

A Living Investigation

The manuscripts are real.The story is fragmentary.

Ancient fragments of a lost story about giants survived among the Dead Sea Scrolls. Some words can still be read. Others have been reconstructed by scholars. This investigation separates what survives from what has been restored, interpreted—and reconstructed for our films.

Book of Giants · Qumran Cave 4

Physical manuscript

What it proves

Ancient manuscript fragments belonging to a work scholars identify as the Book of Giants physically survive among the Dead Sea Scrolls.

What it doesn't prove

The surviving manuscripts do not establish that giants historically existed or that the supernatural events narrated in the ancient story occurred.

A surviving fragment is not a complete story.

What survives

4Q203 Fragment 8 preserves material associated with a second tablet or letter connected with Enoch. It addresses the Watchers and refers to deeds and corruption involving them and their offspring.

[Restored text]

The fragment is damaged. Portions of its destruction and judgment language have been editorially reconstructed by scholars; brackets in critical readings must not be silently removed.

Scholarly interpretation

Scholars understand Enoch's message as announcing condemnation, punishment, or destruction involving the Watchers and giants. The complete interpretation of Ohyah's dream does not survive intact.

Artist reconstruction

The companion Reel turns this fragmentary textual material into a continuous dramatic encounter. Its dialogue and physical staging are not presented as a verbatim translation.

Record mapping: the IAA labels B-295415 “Plate 906, Frag 4” and identifies it as 4Q203. Marro's critical edition maps that imaged object to manuscript fragment 8.

The Book of Giants — Chapter III Investigation

Did the giants really pray for mercy?

They were told to pray. But the verdict had already been given.

So what was the prayer for?

The Reel shows the giants confronting judgment. But the surviving Aramaic fragments complicate the story. One damaged passage from 4Q203 preserves a command translated “…and pray.”

That sounds like an answer. It isn't.

Before asking whether the giants prayed for mercy, we first have to determine who was actually being told to pray.

Watch Chapter III ▶Open the first receipt ↓

Evidence plate 1 · The receipt

The command survives.

Direct textual evidence

4Q203 is an Aramaic manuscript of the Book of Giants from Qumran Cave 4. Fragment 8, line 15 preserves the plural command commonly translated “…and pray.”

The command to pray really is part of the surviving textual evidence.

But the command is not securely addressed to Ohyah, Hahyah, or the giant offspring. The textual context associates it with Shemihazah and his fellow Watchers.

The first correction: the prayer command may not have been directed to the giants at all.

Evidence plate 2 · The correction

Not the giants. Their fathers.

The Watchers are the heavenly beings who descended and fathered the giant offspring in the Enochic tradition. Shemihazah is associated with the Watchers. Ohyah and Hahyah belong to the next generation: the giant sons.

WatchersHeavenly fathers
↓
Giant sonsThe next generation

Textual / Enochic traditionShemihazah → Ohyah / Hahyah

Watchers and giants are related. They are not the same category of being.

If the command to pray concerns the fathers, what were the giant sons doing while judgment closed in?

Evidence plate 3 · Fear

The giants saw their end coming.

Textual evidence · fragmentary context

The surviving Book of Giants fragments portray Hahyah and Ohyah receiving frightening dreams or visions. Their meaning points toward destruction and judgment, although damaged wording and editorial reconstruction must remain visible.

No surviving dream proves that the giants prayed, and the gaps do not license invented dialogue.

The giants now know something is wrong.

Their physical scale has not changed.Their confidence has.

But fear alone did not make these beings powerless. The text remembers just how powerful they believed themselves to be.

Evidence plate 4 · The limit of strength

He could defeat mortals. But not what was above him.

4Q531 · first-person boast

A surviving first-person speaker boasts of extraordinary strength against mortal opponents, then acknowledges that heavenly opponents are stronger.

This boast should not be assigned to Ohyah. The text subsequently shifts to “Then Ohyah said to him…,” which makes the preceding voice someone else. Matthew Goff, following a proposal by Émile Puech, associated that speaker with Gilgamesh; Stuckenbruck left the identity unresolved. Gilgamesh therefore remains a scholarly reconstruction, not secure manuscript wording.

Mortals could be overpowered. Heaven could not.

They hadn't lost their strength.They had discovered its limit.

Once strength could no longer solve the problem, the story turns toward someone physically insignificant by comparison. A human. Enoch.

Evidence plate 5 · Related Enochic tradition

Angels needed a human to speak for them.

Related Enochic tradition — 1 Enoch 12–16

In the Book of Watchers, the condemned Watchers ask the human Enoch to prepare and carry their petition. Their appeal concerns forgiveness and their fate, with their offspring implicated in the consequences of judgment. The divine verdict is not reversed.

Book of Giants

Separately, the giants seek Enoch's knowledge or interpretation, with Mahaway functioning as messenger in the surviving narrative. These are related traditions, not the same surviving scene.

Heaven → Enoch → WatchersWatchers → Enoch → HeavenGiants → Mahaway → Enoch
The Watchers came down from heaven.Enoch was taken up.The beings who came from heaven needed a human who could reach it.

Interpretive framing: Angels were supposed to speak for humans. These angels needed a human to speak for them.

But one final piece of evidence makes the original question harder to answer. Because a prayer actually survives.

Evidence plate 6 · The unsolved prayer

A prayer survives. But who prayed it?

Textual evidence · speaker disputed

4Q203 Fragments 9–10 contain prayer language addressed to God. The identity of the speaker, however, is a textual and scholarly problem.

The fragments do not securely identify Ohyah, Hahyah, or an unnamed giant as the speaker. Marro's 2026 critical edition discusses the material in connection with Enoch's intercessory role; Stuckenbruck's earlier commentary leaves the speaker and placement unresolved. The fragmentary state and the history of arrangement must remain visible.

  • A command to pray survives.
  • A prayer survives.
  • Fear and distress survive.
  • The giants seek Enoch.
  • Bowing and weeping language survives elsewhere in 4Q203.

No surviving Qumran passage securely says “Ohyah prayed for mercy.”

Investigation verdict

So—did the giants really pray for mercy?

The surviving fragments don't tell us for certain.

The Book of Giants preserves fear, approaching judgment, appeals to Enoch, humbling imagery, a command to pray, and surviving prayer material. What it does not securely preserve is a passage identifying Ohyah—or another named giant—as the speaker of a prayer for mercy.

The possibility is present.The identification is not.

Absence of proof is not proof that no giant ever prayed. Fragmentary prayer material is not proof that one did.

Human meaning

They hadn't lost their strength.They had lost their standing.

The Book of Giants does not become interesting because enormous beings suddenly become physically weak. It becomes interesting because physical power stops mattering.

Dreams reveal what strength cannot prevent. Judgment establishes a boundary they cannot cross. And beings who once dominated mortals are left seeking answers from a human scribe.

The Book of Giants · Chapter IV

Sins of the Father

Did fallen angels really ask a human to speak for them?

Ancient writings preserve a remarkable reversal. Heavenly beings descended to earth, took human wives, and fathered children remembered as giants. But when judgment approached, these powerful beings could not save themselves—or their sons.

They turned to a human. His name was Enoch.

“You should intercede for men, and not men for you.”

1 Enoch 15:2 · R. H. Charles translation

Why would beings from Heaven need a human to speak for them?

Movement IV

The Giants Died.

But death didn't end their story.

Section 12 · 1 Enoch 15–16 · Enochic tradition

Something remained.

The giants have an unusual origin in the story: heavenly spirits and human flesh. After their physical deaths, 1 Enoch claims that evil spirits proceeded from their bodies.

“Evil spirits have proceeded from their bodies.”

1 Enoch 15:9 · R. H. Charles translation

The text then speaks of the “spirits of the giants” as remaining upon the earth and bringing affliction and destruction.

Keep the categories separate: the Watchers are not the spirits of the dead giants. This is the claim of the Enochic narrative, not proof of supernatural events.
